What your child does

Every session pairs two things kids love: building something real with their hands, and making it come alive with code. Children build working models from LEGO Education kits — the icon-coding SPIKE Essential set (45345) for our youngest, and the SPIKE Prime platform (45678) for older bands — then program what they built to sense, move, and respond.

Coding meets kids exactly where they are. Pre-readers in K–2 program with icon-only blocks — no reading required, just logic they can see and drag. As kids grow, they move to word blocks and then toward real text-based logic: variables, conditions, loops, and debugging. The LEGO always stays in their hands; the code is how they give it a brain.

How we talk about AI — honestly

We will be straight with you, because a lot of programs are not. Across all of grades K–8, kids explore how AI works — what it is, how a machine can "learn" from examples, and where it shows up in daily life. They are not building or training their own AI, and we will never tell you they are.

Only our older bands — grades 3–5 and grade 6 — actually train a simple model, and even then it's a small, supervised classroom activity. They use Google's Teachable Machine, which trains right in the web browser on the device in front of them. There is no login or account, and the images kids use are not stored anywhere unless they choose to save them. It's a safe, transparent way to see a model learn — and just as importantly, to see its limits.

Taught from first principles

We don't teach kids to memorize the names of apps. We teach them what's actually happening underneath, so they can reason about anything they meet later. Two ideas anchor the whole program: how a computer makes a decision — step by step, using conditions and loops — and how an AI model learns a pattern from examples instead of being told a rule.

A concrete example: a child trains Teachable Machine to tell a red brick from a blue brick by showing it a handful of pictures of each. When it suddenly guesses wrong on a brick held in shadow, that's the lesson — the model only knows what it was shown. The child just discovered why AI needs good examples, in a way no lecture could teach. From there, they can reason about far bigger AI systems, because they understand the engine.

  • No prior coding experience needed — complete beginners welcome
